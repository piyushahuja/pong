Trained policy

What the policy learned to look for

Each of the 200 hidden unit holds 6,400 weights — one for every pixel of the court. Laid back out as an 80×80 picture, almost every unit turns out to be a ball trajectory. Here each unit is drawn as the path it watches for, floating at the height of the vote it casts. The shadow each one throws on the floor is the picture it was extracted from.

▸ Start here — what am I looking at?

The flat surface is the Pong court, seen from above. Each curve floating over it belongs to one neuron inside the trained network, and traces the ball path that neuron responds to most strongly.

Height is not position — it is the vote that neuron casts. Green curves float above because they argue for UP; blue hang below because they argue for DOWN. The faint sheet between them is the level a neuron would sit at if it voted for nothing.

▸ Try this
  1. Watch the two layers separate as the scene turns. Notice the green curves and the blue curves slope in opposite directions — one set follows rising balls, the other falling ones.
  2. Press votes UP only, then votes DOWN only, to see each committee's paths on their own.
  3. Press drop to the floor. Every curve sinks onto the court and meets its own shadow — the shadow was the flat weight picture it came from all along.
  4. Drag to orbit. From directly above, the curves become the ball trajectories they represent.
▸ What this is evidence of

Nobody wrote any of this down. The network was shown a hundred thousand numbers a frame and told, occasionally and long afterwards, +1 or −1. Out of that it arrived at paths across the court, a notion of which way the ball is travelling, and a division of its 200 units into two roughly equal camps — 101 for UP, 99 for DOWN, with none abstaining.

One caution the picture can mislead you about: a neuron's curve is its favourite path, not its only one. It still fires about half the time when the ball is nowhere near it — just less hard. The companion demo measures that.
