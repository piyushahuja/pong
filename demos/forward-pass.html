▸ Start here — what am I looking at?

A trained neural network playing Pong, frozen so you can watch a single frame travel through it. The scene reads left to right in three stages:

  1. The court. The game, and what changed since the previous frame.
  2. The 200 bars. The network's hidden units reacting to that change. Bar height is how strongly each one responded.
  3. The two towers. The network adding up its votes — green for UP, blue for DOWN. Whichever is taller wins.

Nothing here is a recording. Every bar and every number is computed in your browser from the real trained weights, and the P(UP) box checks itself against what PyTorch produced.

▸ What each thing on the court means

Grey plates — where the ball and paddles are right now. Shown to help you follow the game. The network never sees this. Press show screen to remove it.

Amber cube — a pixel that just lit up. Charcoal cube — a pixel that just went dark. These two are the input: the network is handed the current frame minus the previous one, and nothing else. A moving ball shows up as an amber dot where it arrived and a charcoal dot where it left.

Your paddle — the short dark block on the right, sliding up and down. The thin rail beneath it is the column it is stuck in. The cone beside it points the way the network wants to move, and grows as the network gets more certain.

Thin lines on the court — the favourite paths of the units shouting loudest this frame. The ball is usually sitting on or near them.

▸ Try this, in order
  1. Press pause, then step a few times. Watch an amber and a charcoal cube leapfrog across the court. That pair is the ball.
  2. Press show screen to drop the grey layer. What is left is all the network ever gets.
  3. Press loudest right now. The single unit contributing most opens in the panel below: its picture, its vote, and what it is doing this instant.
  4. Walk sideways with ◀ and ▶. Notice each unit has a different favourite path, and that neighbouring units are unrelated — the order means nothing.
  5. Look at the small curve under the unit's picture. The flat stretches are frames where it was switched completely off.
  6. Drag the slider to the end of the rally. The vote spikes as the ball comes home.
  7. Press bars: activation to switch to contribution. Some tall bars shrink — they fire loudly but their vote carries little weight.
▸ Four things people get wrong

“It only pays attention once the ball is near my paddle.”

No. 106 units fire with the ball at the opponent's end, 100 at yours — flat across the whole court. What rises near your paddle is only confidence, mean |logit| 5.1 → 7.1. It watches the ball the whole way.

“A unit only fires when the ball is on its line.”

No. A unit fires 49–51% of the time at every distance from its own line. The line changes how hard it fires — 0.42 on it against 0.26 far away, about 1.6×. A preference, not a switch.

“The units that aren't firing are nearly off.”

They are exactly zero. The activation function keeps positives and flattens everything else to nothing, so about half the network contributes precisely zero to the vote each frame — a different half each time. That is the grey column in the histogram.

“Shouldn't UP and DOWN be left and right?”

The paddles slide vertically, so UP and DOWN are right. Oddly, the game environment disagrees — it reports the two actions as RIGHT and LEFT, left over from an arcade cabinet whose joystick was mounted sideways. The action it calls RIGHT moves the paddle up.

▸ Words used here
Policy
The network itself: screen in, choice of move out.
Hidden unit
One of the 200. Each holds a picture the same size as the screen and reports how well the current change matches it.
Activation (h)
How strongly a unit responded this frame. Never negative — anything below zero becomes zero.
Vote weight (w₂)
A fixed number per unit, decided during training. Positive means it argues for UP, negative for DOWN. It never changes while you watch.
Contribution (w₂h)
Vote weight times activation. What that unit actually adds to the decision this frame.
Logit
Every contribution added up. One number. Positive leans UP.
P(UP)
The logit squashed into a probability. The move is then drawn at random from it, which is how the network keeps trying things instead of always repeating itself.
▸ Where these numbers come from

The weights are a real checkpoint, trained for 109,100 games to a running score of +8.2 against the built-in opponent. The rally is one point it actually won.

The weights are shrunk to 8-bit numbers so the page stays small. The cost of that is tiny and the page shows it: the P(UP) box prints the browser's answer beside PyTorch's, and the gap is never more than 0.003.

The claims in “four things people get wrong” were measured over 2,050–3,310 frames of fresh play, not taken from this one rally.

All 200 activations, this frame

Half the bars land in the single column at zero. That column is not “nearly off” — it is exactly 0, contributing exactly nothing to the vote.

The output is one number: logit = Σ w₂ᵢ hᵢ, the green committee's total minus the blue committee's. Sigmoid turns it into a probability and the action is sampled from it, which is what makes the policy explore. Both committees are loud the whole time; the output is the small residue left when you subtract one from the other, which is why the vote flips sharply while the activity behind it barely moves.
