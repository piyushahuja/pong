The generative question

Where does the contract break?

Everything in Lecture 1 ran on one arrangement: w ← w + η(t − y)x — and t, the right answer, was put there by a person. Sixty thousand images, sixty thousand human decisions.

So put four problems side by side and ask, for each one, whether a person could write that answer down.

Is this handwritten digit a 7? Yes. Any human labels it in a second.
Is there a pedestrian in this frame? Yes. Slower, but a person can draw the box.
What is the best move in this Go position? Nobody knows. The strongest player alive can give you an opinion, and opinions are what we are trying to beat.
How should this robot arm move, right now, to pick up that cup? Nobody can write it down. You can say whether the cup ended up in the hand.

For the bottom half of that table there is no t. There is only, much later and much vaguer: that went well, or that went badly.
Instructive feedback hands you the answer. Evaluative feedback hands you a verdict — and for these problems it is the only kind there is.

Open by putting the update rule back up and asking where t comes from. "The dataset." Where does the dataset come from? Pause. A person sat down and wrote it. That is the contract, and it takes thirty seconds now that slide 05 has gone. Then install the vocabulary on the way past — instructive versus evaluative, which is the distinction Sutton and Barto open their textbook on. The version that bites: I mark your essay four out of ten. You now know you did badly. You still do not know what a good essay was, and you do not know which of the twenty things you did was the bad one. Run the table as a poll, row by row, hands up. The first two are unanimous. The third is where the room splits, and the split is the lesson: several students will say "ask a professional", and the answer is that a professional's move is by definition at most professional-strength. If the label is a human opinion, the ceiling is human. The fourth row is the one to linger on, because it is not about games. Nobody can write down the joint angles. Everybody can see whether the cup got picked up. That asymmetry — cheap to evaluate, impossible to specify — is the entire reason reinforcement learning exists.
2010
Hassabis · Legg · Suleyman

Games, and what he thought they were for

Hassabis was a serious chess player very young, somewhere near the top of his age group in the world. He was learning to program at the same time.

He has described reading a programming book in Foyles at about eleven, then going home and writing an Othello opponent on an Amiga. Othello rather than chess, because chess was more than the machine could manage. The program beat his younger brother.

What is my brain doing when I solve these problems, and could a machine do the same thing?

A game is small enough to simulate and hard enough to be interesting. You can run it a million times, and you always know who won. That is what he thought games were: small universes you could study intelligence inside.

WHY REINFORCEMENT LEARNING, OF ALL THINGS

He did a PhD in cognitive neuroscience first, deliberately, as reconnaissance. One finding came back mattering more than the rest.

Dopamine neurons in the midbrain appear to signal prediction error: the gap between the reward you expected and the reward you got. That is the same quantity a temporal-difference learning algorithm runs on.

So the argument was not that RL was fashionable. In 2010 it emphatically was not. The argument was that something in the one working example of general intelligence appears to compute exactly this, and it would be strange to ignore that.

We come back to the dopamine result properly in the second half, where it is the hinge between the psychology and the algorithm. For now it is enough to know it is the reason this company was built on reinforcement learning and not on something else.
The biography is only here to carry two things, so do not let it become an anecdote reel. First, what games were for. Everyone assumes DeepMind did games because games are a good demo. That is backwards. Games were the laboratory: cheap to run, impossible to argue with about who won, and small enough that you could do the experiment a million times before lunch. Twenty years on, that is still how the place ran experiments. Second, the dopamine link, which is the one that makes the bet look considered rather than romantic. Say the shape of it now and promise the detail later: the brain seems to carry a signal that behaves like a prediction error, and prediction error is the thing TD learning is made of. If someone asks whether that means the brain does RL, the honest answer is no, and it is a better answer than yes. It means one specific signal behaves quantitatively the way a prediction error would. Narrower claim, more impressive for being narrow. You make that point properly on slide 13.

Shane Legg says that was the whole of the first slide. In 2010 you did not write that down in front of investors. The phrase got you filed with cranks, which is roughly why the field had taken to calling itself machine learning instead.

AND UNDERNEATH IT, FIVE THINGS THEY ACTUALLY BELIEVED

01Intelligence can be understood algorithmically. 02General intelligence has to be learned. That is the agent-environment loop, and they wrote it as a bullet point and then spent fifteen years implementing it. Five deserves a remark too. That is the scaling bet, four years before anybody had the phrase, and they were right about it in a way that is slightly annoying. If a student asks who Shane Legg is: the third founder, the one who had been writing about machine superintelligence since his own PhD, and the reason that sentence was on the cover at all.
Part two · The idea itself

What reinforcement learning is

Strip away Go, Breakout and robots and the same shape is left underneath. It is an abstraction of an animal in a world.

At every tick: the agent sees a state, picks an action, and the world replies with a number and a new state. That is the whole interface.

Draw this on the board rather than just showing it, if there is a board. It is three arrows and they should be able to reproduce it from memory for the rest of their lives. Make the cut explicit: everything the agent cannot choose is environment. That includes the agent's own body. A robot's motors are environment; only the decision-making is agent. Students find that counter-intuitive and it is exactly right. Vocabulary is on the next slide. Resist defining anything yet — let them look at the picture first and notice how little is in it. The striking thing about RL is not its complexity but how few moving parts the formulation has.
Five words

The entire vocabulary

State  s What the agent can see right now. For DQN, four consecutive game frames. For a robot, camera and joint angles. Note that this is rarely the whole truth about the world.
Action  a What it can do. For Pong, two things: UP or DOWN.
Reward  r A single number arriving from the environment. Usually zero. In Pong: +1 when you score, −1 when you concede, 0 for the several hundred frames in between.
Policy  π The agent's behaviour: a function from state to action. This is the thing the neural network is. Last week the network mapped image → label; here it maps state → action.
Return  G Not the next reward — the sum of all future rewards, usually with later ones discounted a little. The objective is to maximise this, which is why an agent will accept a loss now for a gain later.
Two of these five carry all the weight. Policy: say explicitly that this is where last week connects. The network is unchanged — multiply, add, squash. What changes is what the output means and where the training signal comes from. If they take one sentence away, that is the one. Return versus reward: this is where sacrifice becomes possible. If the objective were the next reward, no agent would ever give up a piece in chess. Because the objective is the discounted sum, the machine can learn that a loss now buys more later. Every apparently strategic behaviour you have seen today — the Breakout tunnel, move 37 — is this and nothing more. Discounting: gamma near 1 means far-sighted, near 0 means myopic. Karpathy uses 0.99 for Pong. One line, move on.

THE REWARD HYPOTHESIS

All goals can be described by the maximisation of expected cumulative reward.

Silver puts this on a slide in the first lecture of his course and calls it a hypothesis, not a theorem. It is the assumption the whole field is built on, and it is doing enormous work.

Fly a helicopter — +1 for following the intended trajectory, −100 for crashing.

Beat a world champion — +1 for winning, −1 for losing, 0 for every move in between.

Make a robot walk — +1 per metre travelled, −100 for falling over.

Manage an investment portfolio — reward in pounds.

Do you believe it? Write down the reward function for “be a good doctor”.

Put the challenge at the bottom to the room and give them a minute in pairs. It is the best argument you will have all day and it goes somewhere real. Expect: cure rate. Then push — cure rate rewards refusing hard cases. Patient satisfaction? Rewards prescribing opioids. Survival at five years? Rewards aggressive treatment of people who would have been fine. Every proposal they make is gameable, and the gaming is not hypothetical: this is specification gaming, it is a documented failure mode with a long public list of examples, and it is the same phenomenon as an engagement-maximising feed. The honest position to leave them with: the hypothesis is not obviously true, it is not obviously false, and the difficulty of writing down reward functions for things we actually care about is one of the central open problems in AI safety. That is a real frontier and they can work on it.
1898
Edward Thorndike

Where the idea actually comes from

Thorndike put cats in a crate that opened when a latch was tripped, and timed how long they took to get out. The first escape was always an accident, arrived at by scrabbling. Put the same cat back in, and the scrabbling gradually narrowed onto the latch.

He plotted escape time against attempt number and got a smooth downward curve — no flash of insight, just a gradual shift in what the animal was likely to do.

Responses followed by satisfaction become more likely; responses followed by discomfort become less likely.

The law of effect, 1911.

Look at what that sentence already contains.

It is selectional, not instructive. Nobody shows the cat the latch. The consequence merely re-weights what it already did.

It is associative. The re-weighting is tied to the situation the animal was in, not applied to its behaviour at large.

It needs exploration. If the cat never scrabbles, the latch is never tripped and there is nothing to reinforce.

Skinner built the next forty years of psychology on this, gave us the word reinforcement, and showed you could shape elaborate behaviour by rewarding successive approximations to it.

The name of the field is a direct inheritance. Sutton and Barto took the term from animal psychology, and the debt is acknowledged openly in the textbook — the first chapter is partly a history of exactly this.
The cat is the best teaching object in the lecture because every claim in the formalism is visible in it. Walk the three bullets slowly and point back at the animal for each one. The exploration bullet is the one to dwell on, because it is a design constraint rather than an observation. A policy that has stopped trying new things cannot improve, ever — not because the algorithm is weak but because the information does not exist. That is the exploration-exploitation problem and it arrives later in the lecture. One caution worth voicing, if the room is strong: the analogy to real animals is genuinely useful and genuinely limited. Reinforcement learning is not a theory of how animals learn. It borrowed a principle and then went off and became mathematics.
1997
Schultz, Dayan & Montague

The brain appears to compute δ

Wolfram Schultz had been recording from dopamine neurons in monkeys given juice. The firing pattern made no sense as a reward signal, because it kept moving.

Read as a prediction error it made perfect sense. That quantity had a name and an equation already, because Sutton had needed it nine years earlier to make his learning rule work:

δ  =  r  +  V(next)  −  V(now)

What I expected, against what I now expect given what just happened. Read it once and let it go — the algorithm we actually code today does not use it. What matters is that it is a number, and that something in a monkey's midbrain appears to be computing it.

A quantity invented by computer scientists to make an algorithm work turned out to have a measurable physical correlate in the midbrain.

WHAT THE NEURONS DID

Unexpected juice.
A burst of firing. Reward arrived, no prediction preceded it: δ is large and positive.

A tone, then juice, repeatedly.
The burst migrates backwards onto the tone. The juice itself stops producing any response at all — it was predicted, so δ is zero.

Tone, then no juice.
Firing drops below baseline at exactly the moment the juice was due. Expected and absent: δ is negative.

This is the result Hassabis points to when explaining why DeepMind was built on reinforcement learning.

The formula is here only so the three conditions mean something. Do not teach temporal-difference learning — it is a value method, today's notebook is a policy method, and it would be a detour. One sentence: Sutton needed a term for "what I expected minus what I now expect", wrote it down in 1988, and it made his learning rule converge. Deliver the three conditions as three experiments, and ask the room to predict the third before you reveal it. Most people guess "nothing happens". The dip below baseline is the detail that convinces, because a pure reward signal has no reason to go negative when nothing occurs. Something in there is keeping an expectation and subtracting. Then be precise about what this does and does not establish. It does not show the brain runs TD learning. It shows one specific signal behaves, quantitatively, the way a prediction error would. That is a much narrower claim and much more impressive for being narrow. This is also the point to close the loop with slide 07. Hassabis read this literature as evidence that reinforcement learning was not merely a convenient engineering trick but something evolution had already found. That is the intellectual justification for the entire bet.
Slide 8 · David Silver, RL Course, Lecture 1

Four things that make this a different subject

No supervisor Only a reward signal. Nothing ever says what you should have done — only how what you did turned out.
Delayed feedback The consequence of a decision may not show up for hundreds of steps. The blunder and the defeat are separated by an hour of play.
Time matters The data is a sequence, not a pile. Last week's assumption that examples are independent and identically distributed is simply false here.
The agent's actions affect the data it sees Behave badly and you only ever collect bad experience. The dataset is not given to you — you generate it, by acting.

Silver's own framing: reinforcement learning is the science of decision making.

This is essentially Silver's own list and it is worth keeping his order, because the fourth item is the one nobody anticipates and it belongs at the end. Make the fourth concrete. A supervised dataset is fixed: MNIST is MNIST whatever your network does. In RL, a policy that never moves left never sees what is on the left, so it can never learn that the left was better. The model's incompetence censors its own training data. There is no analogue of this in anything they learned last week, and it is why exploration is a first-class problem rather than a detail. If you have time and the room is engaged, Silver's lecture series is the single best next step for them and it is free. Say so here rather than at the end, when they are packing up.
The dilemma that follows

Exploration and exploitation

EXPLOIT

Do the best thing you currently know. Collect the reward you are confident of, and learn nothing new.

EXPLORE

Try something you are unsure about. Probably do worse today, and possibly discover something better than anything you knew.

You cannot do both with the same action, and you must decide afresh at every step, forever. Your usual restaurant or the new one. Your current job or a different career.

The crudest workable answer is ε-greedy: take the best known action, except that some small fraction of the time, act at random. It is almost embarrassingly simple and it is what DQN used.

This slide sells itself if you make it personal before you make it technical. Ask how many of them ordered something new the last time they ate somewhere they like. The room laughs, and then they have the intuition for free. The technical points worth adding: exploiting alone means a policy can be permanently stuck in a mediocre habit, and it will never find out, because the evidence that would correct it is precisely the evidence it declines to gather. And the balance should shift over a lifetime — explore heavily when ignorant, exploit when experienced. Which is a claim about people as much as about algorithms, and they will notice that. Move 37 is the payoff: it exists because the system spent an enormous amount of its training doing things no sensible player would try.

After an hour it returns the ball sometimes.

The claim being made

The paper was not about Breakout

Everyone read that as look, an AI plays Atari. What they meant was considerably harder.

The same learning algorithm can take raw sensory data from many different environments and work out which actions lead to reward, without being rewritten for each one.

Three words in that sentence are carrying it.

SAME

One architecture, one set of hyperparameters. Nothing tuned per game.

RAW

Pixels. Not features some engineer decided were the important ones.

MANY

Forty-nine Atari games, one agent. Above human level on about half of them.

Seven games in the 2013 workshop paper. Forty-nine in Human-level control through deep reinforcement learning, Nature, February 2015.

Sensation → representation → action → a strategy nobody supplied. The whole DeepMind thesis, in a closed world small enough to prove it.

This is the slide that earns the lecture its title, so give it room. Draw the contrast with Deep Blue, which they have all heard of. Deep Blue beat Kasparov in 1997 and could not play draughts, because every single thing it knew about chess was put there by people. DQN plays forty-nine games with the same code because nothing it knows about any of them was put there by people. If someone asks what the network actually predicts, that is a good question and you should hold it. Q-learning is a value method; we teach policy methods today, through Pong. Give them the shape and promise the rest: it learns to predict, for each button, how much total score pressing it now is eventually worth. Be honest about the limits or a sharp student will do it for you. It was enormously sample-hungry. It was bad at anything needing long-horizon planning, Montezuma's Revenge being the standing example. And the accurate figure is above human level on about half, not "beat humans at Atari".

WHY THIS ONE MOVE IS THE WHOLE ARGUMENT

Go has been played for something like four thousand years. Move 37 was not in any of it.

It could not have come from a label. No human knew it, so none could have written it down — that is what the one-in-ten-thousand estimate means.

It came from the system playing itself several million times and keeping what won.

A machine can learn to do a thing that nobody knows how to do.

The emotional centre of the first half. Give it room and do not talk over it. If you have the documentary clip, this is where it goes — around 49:00, ninety seconds either side, and it does more than any diagram will. Set the scene in two sentences and no more: March 2016, Seoul, more than two hundred million people watching, AlphaGo won the match four games to one. On the diagram: AlphaGo was black, the move was at P10, which is the fifth line in from the right edge. The dashed circle is the fourth line, where essentially every book and every professional instinct puts the stone — it is a stronger point for taking territory, and four thousand years of practice says so. Say it is a schematic of the shape rather than the whole board, because it is, and because a real thirty-seven-stone position would be illegible from the back of the room. Michael Redmond called it creative and unique; An Younggil called it a rare and intriguing shoulder hit. Then make the technical point explicit, because the drama alone will not teach anything. AlphaGo's first version did learn from human games. But supervised learning on human games can only ever reproduce the statistics of human play, and in human play this move occurs about once in ten thousand times. The supervised part could not have produced it. What produced it was self-play: the system playing itself millions of times and keeping what won. That is the entire distinction this lecture is about, compressed into one stone. The sentence at the bottom is the first time the room sees it stated outright, and it should arrive as a conclusion rather than a claim — they have just been given the evidence, so say it once, flatly, and stop. It comes back one final time on the last slide before the close. Counterweight, if you want the story honest and the room warmer: game four, Lee Sedol's move 78, the "divine move", which AlphaGo had no answer to. He won that game.
Slide 9 · Karpathy, 2016

Pong from pixels

The agent is not told what Pong is. It never receives ball position = (83, 42) or paddle_y = 97.

It receives what a camera would receive: a grid of pixel intensities, 210 × 160 × 3, which is 100,800 numbers, thirty times a second.

It has two choices — UP or DOWN. It gets +1 when it scores, −1 when it concedes, and 0 for the several hundred frames in between.

WHAT IT IS NEVER TOLD

that some pixels constitute a ball
that other pixels are its paddle
that objects persist between frames
that the ball has a velocity
that the ball bounces
that a paddle can intercept it
that moving up now can matter fifty frames later
what a good move is

Pong, and the MDP it really is — Karpathy (2016)

Left is what you see. Right is what the problem actually is: a graph whose nodes are game states and whose edges are the actions that move between them, each with some probability. The agent never sees the graph. It sees the left-hand picture, one frame at a time, as a hundred thousand numbers.

Slow right down here. This slide is where the whole lecture becomes concrete and it deserves four or five minutes on its own. Read the right-hand column aloud, line by line. Each line is a fact the room assumed the machine had, and each one has to be discovered. Most students do not realise, until they see this list, that "there is a ball" is itself something learned. The number to hold onto is 100,800 in, two out. Everything between those two is the learning problem.

PUT YOURSELF IN ITS POSITION

I hand you this, thirty times a second:

[0, 0, 0, 144, 144, 0, 0, 0, … 100,000 more numbers …]

And I say only: choose A or B.

Then occasionally, much later: +1.

I never explain any of it. Go on then.

Deliver this one straight and then be quiet for a good five seconds. The silence does the work. If you want to push it further, ask how long they think they would need before they even suspected that some of those numbers were an object that moved. That is what the network is doing in its first thousand games.

The hard problem

Which action earned the point?

t = 1     UP       reward 0
t = 2     UP       reward 0
t = 3     DOWN     reward 0
t = 4     DOWN     reward 0
      ...
t = 47    UP       reward 0
      ...
t = 80    UP       reward 0
t = 81             reward +1

Eighty decisions. One number, at the end.

Some of those actions were the reason. Most were irrelevant. A few were probably mistakes that happened to be survivable.

The reward does not say which. And you are adjusting roughly a million parameters on the strength of it.

This is the credit assignment problem, and it is the thing that makes reinforcement learning hard in a way nothing last week was hard.

Four games. Two won, two lost — and the algorithm encourages every action in the winners. Karpathy (2016)

Ask them how they would solve it before you tell them anything. You will get three answers, and all three are real research programmes: reward the most recent actions most (that is discounting), work out what would have happened otherwise (counterfactuals and baselines), or learn to predict value at every step so you never wait for the end (that is TD, which you did twenty minutes ago — point back at it). Then contrast with last week in one sentence, because the contrast is the point: with backpropagation the error is available at the output on every single example, so credit assignment through the layers is a calculus exercise. Here the error is one number, arriving late, for an entire episode.

The whole machine

Two layers. That is the entire thing.

Karpathy's policy network (2016)

6400 inputs, 200 hidden units, one output read as the probability of moving UP. About 1.3 million parameters, one hidden layer, no convolutions. It is smaller than anything you built last week.

Worth saying plainly: this is the same object as Lecture 1. Same layers, same weights, same backpropagation. Only the meaning of the output has changed — last week it was a label, this week it is a decision — and because of that, where the training signal comes from has to change completely.

Deflate it deliberately. Students arrive expecting the architecture to be where the cleverness lives, and here the architecture is a first-week exercise. Everything hard about this problem is in where the gradient comes from, not in the shape of the net. Point at the flattening again if you did not before: turning the 80x80 image into a 6400-vector destroys the fact that adjacent pixels are adjacent, and the network has to rediscover that too. A convolutional net would not have to. Karpathy uses a plain one on purpose, to show the crudest possible thing still works. If somebody asks why only one output rather than two: with two actions you only need the probability of one of them. It generalises to a softmax over however many buttons the console has.
Williams, 1992

The REINFORCE algorithm

Or, in plain words: a comically crude answer that works

Play a whole game, whatever your current policy says.

If the outcome was better than usual, make every action you took a little more likely.

If it was worse than usual, make them all a little less likely.

Repeat a few hundred thousand times.

It reinforces the good actions and the irrelevant ones indiscriminately — but the irrelevant ones appear in winning and losing games alike, so their pushes cancel, and the ones that genuinely mattered accumulate. The signal is buried in noise, and the answer is simply to take an enormous number of samples.

Karpathy is honest about the cost: a few days of training on one machine, for a game a person picks up in a minute.

THE SUPERVISED COUNTERPART

Last week's algorithm has a name too, and the two are closer than they look. Supervised classification ascends ∇ log p(y* | x) — push up the probability of the label somebody gave you. That is cross-entropy, trained by SGD.

REINFORCE ascends R · ∇ log p(a | s) — push up the probability of the action you yourself chose, scaled by how the episode turned out.

Same gradient. The label has been replaced by your own sample, and the certainty of a teacher by a number that arrives at the end.

The cancellation argument is the one insight on this slide and it repays being said twice, in different words. An action that has nothing to do with the outcome shows up in good episodes and bad episodes at roughly the same rate, so over many episodes it gets pushed up as often as down. An action that genuinely tends to precede winning gets pushed up more often than down. Nobody assigns credit; the statistics do it, slowly. On the name: REINFORCE is Ronald Williams, 1992, and it is a forced acronym — reward increment equals nonnegative factor times offset reinforcement times characteristic eligibility. Nobody uses the expansion. Say it once for the record and move on, but do give them the name, because it is what the method is called in every paper they will read and "policy gradients" is the family, not the algorithm. The supervised comparison in the box is the one to put on the board if you have one. Write the two gradients under each other and point at the only difference. In supervised learning the thing multiplying the gradient is a one, because you were told the answer and you are certain of it. In REINFORCE it is the return, which might be minus one, and which you do not find out until the end. Everything difficult about this subject is contained in that substitution. That also explains the sample inefficiency plainly, and sample inefficiency is the honest headline criticism of the whole field. A human learns Pong in a minute. This takes days. Ask why, and let them speculate — the answers involve prior knowledge of objects and physics that the network has to build from nothing, and it is an open research question, not a settled one.
The same picture, twice

Where the gradient comes from

Supervised. The label says UP. Backpropagate the gradient that makes UP more likely, immediately, for every frame. The multiplier on that gradient is 1, because somebody knew.

Policy gradients. No label. So sample an action, compute the identical gradient, and hold it — until the end of the game decides whether to add it or subtract it. Diagrams: Karpathy (2016).

Put these two up together and say almost nothing. The visual point is that the machinery is identical: same network, same forward pass, same backward pass. The only difference is the number the gradient gets multiplied by before it is applied. Upstairs, that number is 1 and it arrives instantly, because a person wrote the answer down. Downstairs it is the return, it might be negative, and it does not arrive for another eighty frames. That is the entire lecture, in two diagrams. If a student has followed nothing else, this is the slide to send them away with. Worth naming the consequence: because the downstairs multiplier is noisy, you need enormously more samples to average the noise out. Sample inefficiency is not a flaw in the implementation. It is the price of not having a teacher.

Being honest about it

Four things wrong with all of this

1 · IT IS ABSURDLY SLOW

Karpathy's Pong takes days on one machine. You learn it in a minute. The gap is prior knowledge — you already know about objects, motion and bouncing, and the network builds all of it from scratch, every time.

2 · THE REWARD IS A LIE YOU WRITE

A boat-racing agent found it scored more by spinning in circles collecting pickups than by finishing the race. It was not broken. It maximised exactly what it was given. Every reward function anyone has written for an open-ended task has been gameable.

3 · IF NOTHING SCORES, NOTHING LEARNS

Montezuma's Revenge: climb down, fetch the key, open the door. DQN scored zero. Random flailing never once reaches the key, so there is never anything to reinforce. Thorndike's cat only escapes because the crate is small.

4 · IT NEEDS A WORLD IT CAN RUIN

Millions of episodes assume failure is free. It is free in Atari and free in Go. It is not free in a car, a hospital, or a portfolio — and the places we most want this are exactly the places you cannot fail a million times.

The pattern underneath all four: this method is spectacular wherever the world can be simulated cheaply and the outcome scored exactly. Reality is neither.

Do not skip this one to save time. Everything they have seen for eighty minutes has worked, and a room that leaves believing reinforcement learning always works has been mistaught. One and three they have already felt — one from the demo, three from Montezuma if you mention it. Two is the one to spend time on, because it is the deepest. The boat is CoastRunners, it is on OpenAI's blog, and if you have thirty seconds of the clip it earns its place; the laugh is worth more than the argument. Then connect it back to slide 11: you asked them to write the reward function for "be a good doctor", and this is what happens when somebody tries. Four is the one they will not have thought of, and it is the reason this is not yet in the world in the way the headlines suggest. Say it plainly: the method needs a world in which failure is free, and the interesting problems are all in worlds where it is not. If the room is strong, the follow-up worth planting: every one of these four is somebody's current research programme, and three of them are unsolved. That is the next slide.
What we did not cover, and where the field is

Everything after today

Learn a value function too Actor–critic. Keep the policy, add a network that predicts how good a state is, and use it as the baseline. Advantage estimation is built from exactly the δ on the dopamine slide. A2C, then PPO — which is what fine-tunes the chatbot you used this morning.
Learn the world itself Model-based RL. Learn to predict what happens next, then plan inside your own model instead of in the real world. Fixes the “failure must be free” problem, if the model is good enough. MuZero, Dreamer, world models.
Make exploration deliberate Curiosity and novelty bonuses. Reward the agent for reaching states it has not seen, rather than waiting for the world to reward it. This is what eventually beat Montezuma's Revenge — not more compute.
Learn without acting Offline RL. Learn a better policy from a fixed log of somebody else's decisions, with no environment to experiment in. The route into medicine, education and anything where you cannot run the experiment.
Get the reward from a person RLHF. Train a model of human preference and use it as the reward. It works, it built the assistants you use — and it reintroduces exactly the ceiling we spent today escaping.

What actually changed between last week and this week is one thing: where the number comes from that tells the machine it was wrong.

LAST WEEK

A person supplies it, for every example, immediately, and it says exactly what the answer should have been.

That is a strong signal, and the machine can be made very good very quickly. It also means the machine can only ever compress what a person already knew, and the label is the ceiling.

THIS WEEK

Lecture 2

Learning Without a Teacher

How a machine learns to do a thing that nobody knows how to do.

The question, again

Lecture one opened on a sentence: there are artifacts you can build out of sand that convert energy into intelligence. It answered half of it — the half about where the intelligence goes once it is in the machine. This lecture is about the other half.

The recap runs through three things you already have. First, the list of what these machines do: read handwritten digits, transcribe speech, find every pedestrian in a frame, read a CT scan, fold a protein, play Go better than anyone alive. Ask of each one whether a person could have written down the right answer, and the list splits cleanly. For the first five, somebody sat down and supplied it. For the sixth, nobody on earth has it.

Second, Cybenko's theorem: one hidden layer can approximate any continuous function to arbitrary precision, which is why that list is not surprising. Every item on it is a function from inputs to outputs — camera feed to steering angle, amino acid sequence to atomic coordinates, board state to probability of winning. The theorem promises that a network computing each of them exists.

Which leaves the gap this lecture lives in. A function existing is not the same as a function being teachable. For the CT scan there is a radiologist to say what the answer was. For the winning probability of a Go position there is nobody.

Third, the regression demo. Watch the curve snap onto the points and then look at the points: every one of those dots is an answer somebody supplied. Drag one and the curve follows it, obediently and immediately, to wherever you put it. The network has no opinion about where the dot should be. It has only the dot.

A machine can learn to do a thing that nobody knows how to do.

Where lecture one left us

McCulloch and Pitts gave us a unit that computes and cannot learn. Rosenblatt made it learn, and the whole of his advance sits in one line: w ← w + η(t − y)x. Read the middle term. t is what the answer should have been, y is what the machine said, and the gap between them is a signal that knows which way is wrong. Backpropagation, thirty years later, changes almost nothing about this — it carries the same signed error back through more layers.

Which means every system in lecture one was standing on a pile of answers. MNIST is 60,000 images and 60,000 human-written labels; the intelligence in those labels existed before the network was switched on.

Where that contract runs out

Ask, for four problems, whether a person could write down the correct answer. Is this digit a 7? Yes, in a second. Is there a pedestrian in this frame? Yes, slower, but a person can draw the box. What is the best move in this Go position? Nobody knows — the strongest player alive can give you an opinion, and opinions are what we are trying to beat. How should this arm move, right now, to pick up that cup? Nobody can write it down; you can only say whether the cup ended up in the hand.

For the bottom half of that list there is no target. There is only, much later and much vaguer: that went well, or that went badly.

So there are three kinds of learning signal. Hebb's rule gets no feedback at all and reinforces mistakes as cheerfully as successes. Rosenblatt's rule gets instructive feedback: you are told the answer you should have given, immediately, for every example. Reinforcement learning gets evaluative feedback: one number saying how well it went, not what you should have done instead, and often not until hundreds of decisions later.

2010 — the bet

Demis Hassabis did not begin with a method. He began with the problem of intelligence and treated everything else as instrumental. A serious junior chess player and a programmer at the same time, he has described reading a programming book in Foyles at around eleven and writing an Othello opponent at home because chess was beyond the machine. The question he has repeated since: what is my brain doing when I solve these problems, and could a machine do the same thing?

His route: the brain is the one existing proof that general intelligence is physically possible, so spend years reverse-engineering some of its principles — not copying neurons, but identifying useful computational principles and converting them into algorithms. The principle he singles out as decisive is the link between dopamine prediction-error signals and temporal-difference reinforcement learning. If brains already implement something like RL, building intelligence around RL is not an obviously crazy bet.

Shane Legg's account is that the cover of the 2010 investor plan carried one sentence: build the world's first artificial general intelligence. Hassabis paraphrases it as: step one, solve intelligence; step two, use it to solve everything else. Underneath sat five claims — intelligence can be understood algorithmically; general intelligence requires learning rather than hand-programmed behaviour; the brain offers useful hints; an agent should perceive an environment, learn a representation, act, receive feedback and improve; and increasing compute will make increasingly general learning algorithms practical.

2013 & 2015 — DQN

They did not give it the rules of Breakout, a representation of the paddle, the position of the ball, or an algorithm for where to move. They gave it pixels, the score, and the controller. Then they ran the same architecture across a shelf of different games.

Hassabis's account of watching it: after about thirty minutes, hopeless. After an hour, visibly improving. After a couple of hours, competent. After several hours it dug a tunnel up the side of the wall and put the ball behind the bricks, where it scored on its own. Nobody told it that strategy existed, or that there was a wall, or that there was a behind.

The intended claim was not "an AI plays Atari". It was that the same learning algorithm can take raw sensory data from many different environments and work out which actions lead to reward without being rewritten for each one. The 2013 workshop paper covered seven games; the 2015 Nature paper reported one agent across 49, above human level on about half. Suleyman's line: really the thing that changed everything for us.

2014 — the acquisition

Google announced the purchase in January 2014. Terms were private: contemporary reports put it around £400m or $500m-plus, later reconstructions at roughly $650m. The company was four years old and had shipped no product, only a workshop paper about Atari. A condition of the sale was the creation of an ethics and safety board, which in 2014 was an unusual thing for anybody to insist on.

2015–2018 — AlphaGo

October 2015: AlphaGo beats the professional Fan Hui 5–0, the first time a program had beaten a pro on a full board without a handicap; DeepMind keeps it quiet until publication. January 2016: the Nature paper. March 2016: Lee Sedol, 4–1, watched by more than 200 million people. In game two, move 37 — a shoulder hit on the fifth line where every book says the fourth. The commentators assumed a bug; Lee Sedol left the room and took roughly fifteen minutes over his reply; the move was later judged to have won the game, with one estimate putting the chance of a human playing it at about one in ten thousand. It could not have been trained in. No human label contained it. (Game four is the honest counterweight: Lee Sedol's move 78, the divine move, which AlphaGo had no good answer to.)

October 2017: AlphaGo Zero is given the rules and nothing else, plays itself from random, and within three days beats the version that beat Lee Sedol. It rediscovered centuries of human joseki, played them for a while, then discarded several for something it preferred. The loop — self-play, experience, a better policy, harder games — contains no external supply of data: the system generates its own curriculum. Then AlphaZero ran the same method on chess and shogi and got the same result.

Two institutional bets were now visible: Hinton, Brain and later OpenAI on sequence models, huge corpora and huge compute, learning from what humanity has already written down; Hassabis, Silver and DeepMind on agents, environments and reward, learning from what the machine does and what happens next. They are currently converging — a modern language model is pre-trained the first way and finished the second.

What reinforcement learning actually is

Strip away Go, Breakout and robots and the same shape is left: an agent, an environment, and three arrows. At every tick the agent sees a state, picks an action, and the world replies with a number and a new state. Everything the agent cannot choose is environment — including, for a robot, its own motors.

Five words carry the subject. State: what the agent can see now, which is rarely the whole truth about the world. Action: what it can do — for Pong, UP or DOWN. Reward: a single number from the environment, usually zero. Policy: the agent's behaviour, a function from state to action — and this is the thing the neural network is. Last week the network mapped image to label; here it maps state to action. Return: not the next reward but the discounted sum of all future rewards. That last distinction is where sacrifice becomes possible: if the objective were the next reward, no agent would ever give up a piece in chess.

Underneath sits the reward hypothesis, which Silver states plainly and calls a hypothesis rather than a theorem: all goals can be described by the maximisation of expected cumulative reward. Fly a helicopter: +1 for the intended trajectory, −100 for crashing. Beat a world champion: +1 for winning. Make a robot walk: +1 per metre, −100 for falling. The challenge worth putting to a room: write down the reward function for "be a good doctor". Cure rate rewards refusing hard cases. Patient satisfaction rewards prescribing opioids. Every proposal is gameable, and the gaming is documented and real.

Where the idea came from

In 1898 Edward Thorndike put cats in a crate that opened when a latch was tripped and timed their escapes. The first escape was always an accident; put the same cat back and the scrabbling gradually narrowed onto the latch. Plotted against attempt number, escape time falls smoothly — no flash of insight, just a shift in what the animal was likely to do. His law of effect, 1911: responses followed by satisfaction become more likely, responses followed by discomfort less likely.

That sentence already contains the field. It is selectional rather than instructive — nobody shows the cat the latch, the consequence merely re-weights what it already did. It is associative — the re-weighting is tied to the situation. And it needs exploration — if the cat never scrabbles, the latch is never tripped and there is nothing to reinforce. Skinner built forty years of psychology on it and gave us the word reinforcement.

The computational thread arrives separately. Samuel's draughts program (1959) improved by playing. Bellman (1957) supplied the recursion that mattered: the value of a state is the reward you get now plus the value of the state you end up in. It looks circular and is merely recursive, and it is the same move as a chess player saying a position is good because it leads to good positions. Bellman's method needs a model of the environment — you must know what every action does in every state — which is fine for a small grid and useless for Atari.

Sutton and Barto's contribution, through the 1980s, was that you can get the same recursion out of experience alone, by sampling, with no model.

Temporal difference: learning from a guess

You leave the office at six and estimate you will be home by half past. Ten minutes later you hit stationary traffic and your estimate moves to seven. You have just learned something. You are not home yet, and nobody told you the answer.

What you learned from was the change between two successive predictions. That difference is a usable error signal even though the outcome is an hour away: δ = r + V(next state) − V(this state). What I expected, against what I now expect given what just happened. Nudge the old estimate toward the new one, repeat, and value seeps backwards down a chain of states until a move made forty seconds ago can be credited for a point scored now. The alternative — wait until you get home, then update — works, is called a Monte Carlo method, and throws away everything you learned on the bypass.

1997 — the brain appears to compute δ

Before the result itself, the lineage, because the obvious guess about it is wrong. Reinforcement learning did not begin as an algorithm that biology later happened to match. It began in animal psychology and stayed there for most of a century: Thorndike's crate in 1898, Skinner's reinforcement through the 1930s, and then Rescorla and Wagner in 1972, who formalised the idea that animals learn only when events violate their expectations. That is prediction error stated as psychology, sixteen years before Sutton, and the temporal-difference rule is close to an extension of their model with timing added. Sutton's δ came down that line; what he added was that it could be made to run.

Which is what makes the next step unusual. Lecture 1's tradition began at the cell — Cajal's discrete neurons, abstracted by McCulloch and Pitts into a threshold unit, given a learning rule by Rosenblatt — and it never left that level. This tradition began at the level of the whole animal, was formalised into an algorithm, and then came back to the cell.

Wolfram Schultz had been recording from dopamine neurons in monkeys given juice, and the firing pattern made no sense as a reward signal because it kept moving. Read as a prediction error it made perfect sense. Unexpected juice: a burst of firing. A tone followed repeatedly by juice: the burst migrates backwards onto the tone, and the juice itself stops producing any response, because it was predicted. Tone, then no juice: firing drops below baseline at exactly the moment the juice was due.

The third condition is the convincing one. A pure reward signal has no reason to go negative when nothing happens. Something in there is holding an expectation and subtracting. This does not show the brain runs TD learning; it shows one specific signal behaves, quantitatively, the way a prediction error would — a narrower claim, and more impressive for being narrow. It is the result Hassabis points to when explaining why DeepMind was built on RL.

Four things that make this a different subject

Silver's list. There is no supervisor, only a reward signal. Feedback is delayed, sometimes by hundreds of steps. Time matters — the data is a sequence, not a pile, and the independence assumption from last week is simply false. And the agent's actions determine the data it sees: a policy that never moves left never learns that the left was better. The model's incompetence censors its own training data, which has no analogue in supervised learning and is why exploration is a first-class problem.

Hence the dilemma. Exploit: do the best thing you currently know, collect the reward you are confident of, learn nothing. Explore: try something uncertain, probably do worse today, possibly find something better than anything you knew. You cannot do both with one action and you must decide afresh at every step, forever. The crudest workable answer is ε-greedy — best known action, except that some small fraction of the time, act at random — which is what DQN used. Move 37 exists because the system spent an enormous share of its training doing things no sensible player would try.

Pong from pixels

The agent is not told what Pong is. It never receives ball position = (83, 42) or paddle_y = 97. It receives what a camera receives: 210 × 160 × 3, which is 100,800 numbers, thirty times a second. It has two choices, UP or DOWN. It gets +1 when it scores, −1 when it concedes, and 0 for the several hundred frames in between.

It is never told that some pixels constitute a ball, that other pixels are its paddle, that objects persist between frames, that the ball has a velocity, that it bounces, that a paddle can intercept it, that moving up now can matter fifty frames later, or what a good move is.

Compare how you would have written it. Given ball_x, ball_y, ball_velocity_y and paddle_y, a decent rule is four lines. But notice who did the intelligent work: you supplied the concepts ball, position, velocity, paddle, and you decided which four numbers out of a hundred thousand mattered. With pixel RL you supply image → ??? → ??? → UP/DOWN, and a reward. The ??? has to emerge.

Credit assignment

t = 1     UP       reward 0
t = 2     UP       reward 0
t = 3     DOWN     reward 0
      ...
t = 47    UP       reward 0
      ...
t = 81             reward +1

Eighty decisions, one number at the end. Some of those actions were the reason, most were irrelevant, a few were probably survivable mistakes — and the reward does not say which, while you adjust roughly a million parameters on the strength of it. This is the credit assignment problem, and it is what makes reinforcement learning hard in a way nothing last week was hard. With backpropagation the error is available at the output on every example, so credit through the layers is a calculus exercise. Here the error is one number, arriving late, for an entire episode.

What actually reaches the network

                SHAPE              CONTENT

obs             210x160x3          RGB pixels
                     |  [35:195]
obs_cropped     160x160x3          crop off score + borders
                     |  [::2, ::2, 0]
obs_downsampled  80x80             one channel, every other pixel
                     |  != 144, != 109, != 0
obs_binary       80x80             True / False
                     |  .flatten()
obs_flattened     6400             a vector

Read the right-hand column as deliberate losses: remove irrelevant screen regions, throw away three quarters of the pixels, throw away two colour channels, throw away exact intensities, keep only object-or-background, flatten the picture into a vector. 100,800 numbers become 6,400, and Karpathy's network is then 6400 → 200 → 1 — one hidden layer, one output read as the probability of moving UP. Flattening destroys the fact that adjacent pixels are adjacent; the network rediscovers that too.

Two questions worth asking about this pipeline. Why throw away colour? Because someone looked at the problem and decided it carried no information — a human prior, quietly smuggled into a story about learning from raw pixels. And how can a single frame tell you which way the ball is going? It cannot: the actual input is the difference between consecutive frames, which hands the network motion for free. That is an inductive bias, and it is the clearest example of one you will find.

The algorithm

Play a whole game under your current policy. If the outcome was better than usual, make every action you took slightly more likely. If it was worse, make them all slightly less likely. Repeat a few hundred thousand times. It reinforces good and irrelevant actions indiscriminately — but irrelevant actions appear in winning and losing games alike, so their pushes cancel, while the ones that mattered accumulate. Nobody assigns the credit; the statistics do, slowly. Karpathy is honest about the cost: a few days of training on one machine, for a game a person picks up in a minute.

The same reversal, outside the arcade

Classical robotics: detect the cup, estimate its pose, compute a grasp point, plan a trajectory, execute. Every stage designed, every stage a place to fail. The other way: camera pixels, proprioception and tactile signals into a neural network, out come motor commands, and the only question asked is whether the cup ended up in the hand.

Replace human-designed representation → human-written rules → action with raw sensory data → actions → eventual reward. Be fair to the classical pipeline: it is inspectable, debuggable, and when it fails you know which stage failed. The real argument against it is that it requires somebody to know how to specify each stage, and for most interesting tasks nobody does.

2025 — the argument written down, and then bet on

In April 2025 Silver and Sutton published Welcome to the Era of Experience, a chapter for the MIT Press book Designing an Intelligence. It is the lecture you have just heard, argued as a prediction.

Their frame is three sources of data. Simulation — backgammon, Atari, Go — gave closed worlds with one precise reward, and produced superhuman play that could not leave the box. Human data gave the opposite trade: sweeping generality, and a ceiling, because everything in the corpus was written by a person. Experience is the proposed third: data the agent generates by interacting with its environment, which has the property that it improves as the agent does, where a fixed corpus does not.

The prosaic reason for the shift is that the good human data is nearly used up — the high-quality sources that can still improve a strong model have mostly been consumed, and progress from supervised learning on them is measurably slowing. The interesting reason is the other one: new theorems and new technologies lie outside the boundary of what anyone has written down, so no amount of that corpus reaches them.

They name four things that have to change. Agents must inhabit streams of experience that run for a lifetime rather than a chat turn. Their actions and observations must be grounded in an environment rather than mediated entirely by human dialogue. Their rewards must come from the world rather than from a human's prejudgement of the answer. And they must plan and reason over consequences rather than over chains of English sentences.

The sharpest sentence is aimed at RLHF, from the RL side: human-centric RL "has imposed a new ceiling on the agent's performance: agents cannot go beyond existing human knowledge." RLHF replaced the value function with a human rater, and a human rater cannot score a move that nobody understands. That is move 37 restated as a design constraint, and it is the honest reason a chat model does not produce one.

Their worked example is AlphaProof, which reached silver-medal standard at the International Mathematical Olympiad. It began with roughly a hundred thousand formal proofs written by human mathematicians over many years, then generated a hundred million more by interacting with a formal proving system. The human data was the ignition, not the fuel.

Two hedges are worth carrying. It is a position paper, not an experiment. And its unsolved part is the one the robotics slide already named: rewards in reality are plural and ill-defined, and "ground them in real-world signals" is a direction rather than a method.

Six months later — ineffable

David Silver — who wrote the lecture course, led AlphaGo and AlphaZero, and co-wrote that paper — left DeepMind in January 2026, having founded a company the previous November. In April 2026 it raised $1.1 billion at a $5.1 billion valuation, from Sequoia, Lightspeed, Index, Google and Nvidia, with no product. Sixteen years after a business plan whose cover read build the world's first artificial general intelligence, the same wager, narrower and louder.

Its stated mission is "a superlearner that discovers all knowledge from its own experience, from elementary motor skills through to profound intellectual breakthroughs" — no human data, rediscovering language, science and mathematics, and then continuing past them.

The name carries the argument. Among the company's stated beliefs is that the knowledge such a system acquires "will be too profound to be described by human language". Ineffable: that which cannot be put into words.

A machine can learn to do a thing that nobody knows how to do.

That was the first line of the lecture. Move 37 was one move that no human could have supplied and that none could entirely explain. The bet now is that everything left worth discovering sits on that side of the language — and it is a bet, not a result. The method is spectacular wherever the world can be simulated cheaply and the outcome scored exactly, and reality is neither. Whether "discover all knowledge from experience" survives contact with a domain that has no reward function is the open question of the subject, and it is genuinely open.
